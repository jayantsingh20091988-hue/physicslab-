START WITH WHY

Why Do We Need Rockets?

🌍

Earth Has Gravity

Earth continuously pulls objects toward its centre. A spacecraft near Earth therefore needs enough velocity to move along its desired trajectory.

WHY? Gravity keeps trying to bend the spacecraft's path back toward Earth.
🚀

Rockets Change Velocity

A rocket produces thrust by accelerating exhaust gases in the opposite direction.

WHY? Momentum must be conserved. Gas gets momentum one way, so the rocket gains momentum the other way.
🛰️

Satellites Need the Right Motion

A satellite does not simply "float". It is continuously moving while gravity bends its path.

WHY? Its sideways velocity and Earth's gravity work together to create an orbit.
ORBITAL MECHANICS

Why Doesn't a Satellite Fall to Earth?

↓ Gravity
→ Forward motion
↗ Curved path
01

The satellite is moving sideways

It already has velocity parallel to Earth's surface.

02

Gravity pulls it inward

Earth's gravity constantly changes the direction of its velocity.

03

The path bends

Instead of travelling in a straight line, the satellite follows a curved trajectory.

04

That curved free-fall is an orbit

The satellite is falling toward Earth while its sideways motion continuously carries it forward.

MATHEMATICS

Orbital Velocity

For a simplified circular orbit around a spherical body:

v = √(GM / r)
G Universal gravitational constant
M Mass of the central body
r Distance from the centre
💡
WHY does orbital speed decrease at larger radius?

Farther from Earth, gravity is weaker. Therefore a circular orbit at a larger radius requires less orbital speed.

SATELLITE ORBITS

Different Orbits, Different Jobs

🛰️
LEO

Low Earth Orbit

Relatively close to Earth. Common for Earth observation, scientific missions and many modern satellite networks.

~160–2,000 km
🌎
MEO

Medium Earth Orbit

Higher than LEO. Navigation satellite systems commonly operate in this region.

~2,000–35,786 km
📡
GEO

Geostationary Orbit

A special equatorial orbit where the satellite's orbital period matches Earth's rotation.

~35,786 km
🌐
POLAR

Polar Orbit

Passes near Earth's poles and can allow broad coverage as Earth rotates underneath.

Near-polar inclination

ROCKET SCIENCE

How Does a Rocket Move?

The surprising answer: it does not need to push against air.

01

Fuel burns

Chemical energy is converted into thermal and kinetic energy of the exhaust.

02

Exhaust is accelerated backward

The engine gives the exhaust gases momentum in the backward direction.

03

The rocket gains opposite momentum

Conservation of momentum gives the rocket momentum in the opposite direction.

04

So the rocket accelerates

This works even in space because the rocket is not pushing against the surrounding air.

III
Newton's Third Law

When the rocket pushes exhaust gases backward, the gases exert an equal and opposite interaction on the rocket.

DEEPER PHYSICS

Escape Velocity

How fast would an object need to move to escape Earth's gravity without further propulsion?

ve = √(2GM/R)
≈ 11.2 km/s Earth's surface escape speed
≈ 7.9 km/s Typical circular orbital speed near Earth's surface
🛰️

Orbital velocity

Enough sideways speed for a circular orbit at a chosen radius.

🌌

Escape velocity

The ideal minimum speed needed to escape the gravitational field when starting from that distance, ignoring atmosphere and other losses.

DON'T GET FOOLED

Common Misconceptions

❌

"A satellite has no gravity."

Gravity is still acting strongly on satellites in low orbit.

❌

"A rocket needs air to push against."

Rocket propulsion works through momentum exchange with expelled propellant.

❌

"Zero gravity means no gravity."

Apparent weightlessness in orbit is mainly due to continuous free-fall, not the complete absence of gravity.

❌

"Higher orbit means faster satellite."

For circular Earth orbits, orbital speed decreases as orbital radius increases.

❌

"Escape velocity means escaping instantly."

It is an ideal minimum speed under simplified conditions, not a promise that the object instantly leaves the universe.

❌

"Centripetal force is a new force."

Centripetal force describes the net inward force responsible for circular motion. Gravity, tension or another force can provide it.
