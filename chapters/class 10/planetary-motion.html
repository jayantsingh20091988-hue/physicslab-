START WITH A MYSTERY

Why doesn't Earth fall into the Sun?

This is the question that makes planetary motion interesting instead of just another formula.

☀️

The Sun pulls Earth

The Sun has enormous mass, so it produces a strong gravitational attraction on Earth.

WHY?

Gravity always acts between masses. Since the Sun is extremely massive, its gravitational pull strongly affects Earth's motion.

🌍

Earth is also moving sideways

Earth already has a large tangential velocity. It is not simply sitting still near the Sun.

WHY?

Without an inward force, an object moving sideways would continue approximately along a straight-line path because of inertia.

🌀

Gravity bends the path

Earth's forward motion tries to carry it onward, while gravity continuously changes its direction toward the Sun.

THE KEY IDEA

Gravity does not need to completely stop Earth. It continuously changes the direction of its velocity, producing an orbit.

CONCEPT

What is planetary motion?

Planetary motion is the motion of planets around a central star due to gravitational interaction.

What?

A planet travels around the Sun along an orbital path.

How?

The planet has velocity while gravity continuously changes the direction of that velocity.

Why?

The combination of the planet's motion and the Sun's gravitational attraction produces a bound orbital motion.

UNDERSTAND THE ORBIT

Orbit: not just a circular track

Many textbook diagrams show circles because they are easy to draw. Real planetary orbits are generally elliptical.

☀
🌍

What is an ellipse?

An ellipse is like a stretched circle. It has two special points called foci.

In the simplified planetary model, the Sun lies at one focus of the ellipse.

WHY DOES THIS MATTER?

The distance between a planet and the Sun can change during the orbit. That helps explain why orbital speed is not always identical.

KEPLER'S DISCOVERY

Before the laws came the observations.

Johannes Kepler studied detailed observations of planetary positions. Instead of forcing the planets to fit a perfect circle, he looked for mathematical patterns that actually matched the observations.

Observations → Patterns → Mathematical Laws → Later explained by Newton
01
KEPLER'S FIRST LAW

The Law of Orbits

Planets move in elliptical orbits with the Sun at one focus of the ellipse.

What did Kepler discover?

Planetary paths are not generally perfect circles. They are better represented by ellipses.

WHY?

Kepler was trying to match mathematical models with actual astronomical observations. A perfect circle could not explain the observed positions accurately enough.

Think about it

If planets were always exactly the same distance from the Sun, a circular model would be enough. But the observed Sun-planet distance changes.

☀ Sun
🌍

The Sun is at one focus of the ellipse.

02
KEPLER'S SECOND LAW

The Law of Areas

A line joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.

⚡

Near the Sun

The planet moves faster.

Higher orbital speed
↔
🐢

Far from the Sun

The planet moves more slowly.

Lower orbital speed
WHY DOES THE SPEED CHANGE?

As the planet moves through its elliptical orbit, its distance and direction relative to the Sun change. The Sun's gravity continuously changes the planet's velocity. Kepler's observations revealed the precise equal-area pattern.

The important point is that the planet does not travel equal distances in equal times. Instead, it sweeps equal areas.

KEPLER'S THIRD LAW

The Law of Periods

For planets orbiting the same central star, T² is proportional to a³.

T² ∝ a³
T = orbital period a = semi-major axis

Orbital speed is lower

In the circular-orbit approximation, v = √(GM/r), so larger r means lower orbital speed.

→
03

Period becomes longer

More distance plus lower orbital speed means the planet needs more time to complete one orbit.

EXPLORE DEEPER

Where does orbital speed come from?

For a simplified circular orbit, gravity supplies the inward force required to continuously bend the motion.

1

Gravitational force

F = GMm/r²
↓
2

Required centripetal force

F = mv²/r
↓
3

Equate the forces for a circular orbit

GMm/r² = mv²/r
↓
Therefore v = √(GM/r)

Larger orbital radius → lower circular orbital speed.

⚠️ This derivation is for an idealized circular orbit. Real planetary orbits are generally elliptical, so the orbital speed changes along the orbit.

DON'T MEMORIZE THE WRONG IDEA

Common misconceptions

✕

“Planetary orbits are perfect circles.”

Real planetary orbits are generally elliptical, although some can be close to circular.

✕

“Inertia is a force pushing Earth outward.”

Inertia is not a separate outward force. It describes the tendency of motion to continue without a net force.

✕

“Gravity stops acting once orbit begins.”

Gravity is continuously acting and continuously influencing the trajectory.

✕

“Farther planet means faster orbit.”

For circular orbits around the same central mass, greater orbital radius means lower orbital speed.
